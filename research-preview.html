Mercury seen during BepiColombo’s sixth flyby

Mercury · MERTIS · Current

Probing silicate composition and crystallinity on Mercury with BepiColombo.

Silicon is the second-most abundant element in the crusts of rocky planets, behind only oxygen. The mineral phases into which silicon partitions are key indicators of planetary evolution. Identifying these silicate minerals can reveal the nature of the material from which Mercury accreted, as well as the roles of impacts and volcanism in reshaping its surface.

Mercury—the planet closest to the Sun and something of an oddball in our solar system—has a complex history shaped by unusual geochemical processes. By using thermal infrared observations from MERTIS (Hiesinger et al. 2020) to identify the mineral hosts of silicon, I aim to better understand how Mercury formed and evolved.

This project is ongoing (Fall 2026–2028). I look forward to sharing more details through future conference presentations—including EPSC and EGU 2027—and publications.

One second of laser irradiation in a lunar regolith simulant, seen by a thermal infrared camera

Moon · Laser irradiation · Current

Simulating micrometeorite bombardment in lunar regolith using pulsed laser irradiation.

Without a substantial atmosphere to intercept small particles, the surfaces of airless bodies such as the Moon and Mercury are continually bombarded by micrometeorites. The resulting “space weathering” is detectable through remote sensing and directly observable in samples returned from the Moon.

Lasers have a long history as tools for simulating this process (Yamada et al. 1999). Nanosecond laser pulses deposit energy over a timescale similar to a micrometeorite impact, while the pulse energy can be tuned to represent particles of different sizes. By firing many pulses in rapid succession, the cumulative effects of millions of years of space weathering can be reproduced in the laboratory.

At the Technical University of Munich, I work with the VOLARIS team to understand how space weathering affects volatile species such as water. Micrometeorite impacts are highly energetic events that can dissociate water molecules and may help redistribute water across—and into—the lunar surface.

MethodsNanosecond-pulsed laser irradiation · thermal infrared imaging

Nickel detections in Neretva Vallis rocks and their locations along Perseverance’s traverse

Mars · SuperCam & PIXL · Published March 2026

Strong nickel enrichment co-located with redox–organic interactions in Neretva Vallis, Mars.

In 2024, NASA’s Perseverance rover explored Neretva Vallis, an ancient river channel that once carried water into Jezero crater. There, the rover encountered fine-grained sedimentary rocks containing organic matter and reduced sulfur—a remarkable combination of findings previously reported as a potential biosignature (Hurowitz et al. 2025).

SuperCam detected nickel in 32 nearby rocks, with concentrations in individual targets reaching approximately 1.1 weight percent: the highest abundance yet observed in Martian bedrock. We contextualized these enrichments using outcrop-scale imagery and petrographic-scale elemental maps from the PIXL X-ray fluorescence instrument, finding that nickel is concentrated in iron sulfides and their weathering products.

The geochemistry and morphology of these iron sulfides resemble pyrite found in terrestrial Archean and Paleoproterozoic sedimentary rocks. Because nickel is essential to microbial life on Earth, its proximity to reduced sulfur and organic matter strengthens the scientific case for returning Perseverance’s sample from this location. Laboratory analysis on Earth could provide important insights into the complex redox chemistry of early Mars.

LIBS-derived oxide maps and the corresponding region of the lunar meteorite

Moon · LIBS · Published July 2026

LIBS elemental mapping of a feldspathic lunar meteorite: insights for in situ exploration.

New instruments are being developed for future robotic and crewed missions to the Moon. One promising technique is laser-induced breakdown spectroscopy (LIBS), which can measure the elemental composition of rocks and soils directly on a planetary surface. A laser briefly heats a small spot on the sample, creating a plasma whose emitted light carries the signatures of the elements present.

In this study, we developed a detailed calibration that allows LIBS measurements to quantify the composition of lunar materials. The results show that lunar LIBS instruments can detect all major elements, although some—such as potassium—may be difficult to measure at the low concentrations expected on the Moon.

We then used this calibration to produce the first LIBS chemical map of a lunar meteorite. The sample is enriched in aluminium (approximately 29 wt.% Al2O3) and calcium (approximately 14 wt.% CaO), consistent with feldspathic lunar meteorites and an origin in the lunar highlands. These results demonstrate that LIBS imaging can resolve small-scale chemical variations in lunar rocks and support future in situ exploration.

SuperCam plasma diagnostics across observation distances and target types on Mars

Mars · SuperCam LIBS · Published December 2024

LIBS plasma diagnostics with SuperCam on Mars: implications for quantification of elemental abundances.

NASA’s Perseverance rover landed in Jezero crater in 2021 to explore an ancient delta for evidence of past life and collect samples for possible future return to Earth. Its SuperCam instrument uses laser-induced breakdown spectroscopy (LIBS) to quantify the elements in rocks and soils along the rover’s traverse. LIBS is especially valuable for planetary exploration because it works at a distance and across different target types.

Changes in laser irradiance with distance and in laser–target coupling could alter the physical conditions of the plasma plume, potentially affecting SuperCam’s elemental calibration. We investigated these conditions by estimating apparent plasma temperature with multiline Boltzmann plots and electron density through Stark broadening of the H-α line.

Apparent plasma temperature neither decreases with distance nor varies systematically between rocks and soils. The full range measured on Mars is represented in SuperCam’s laboratory calibration dataset, indicating that its elemental calibration is robust and that reliable LIBS observations can be made at distances of at least 8 m. Electron density is approximately 1.4 times higher in soils than in rocks, likely because the H-α line depends on topographic relief—an incompletely understood effect that complicates the quantification of hydrogen in Martian spectra.
